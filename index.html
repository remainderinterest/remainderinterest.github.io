What are remainder interests?
A remainder interest is the deeded right to possess a property, following the end of a life estate. Remainder interests are typically created in estate planning when a person gifts, donates, or sells a home while maintaining the right to live in the home for a lifetime (a ‘life estate’). Unlike a declaration in a will, remainder interests are typically irrevocable and assignable.
What is a life estate?
A life estate is the right to use and occupy a property for a lifetime. When the life estate ends, the property passes along (in ‘as-is’ condition) to the holder of the remainder interest. A life estate may span the lives of two occupants, in which case the life estate will continue for the length of the longer lifetime.
How does the calculator work?
As well as it can! The calculator applies simplified actuarial methods that consider life expectancy, the time value of money (via interest rate assumptions), and the rate of property depreciation. The calculation is similar to those referenced in IRS rules, publications, and guidance but is not an implementation of any tax rule or professional valuation methodology. It should not be relied upon or used in place of professional advice. It only expresses my personal opinion on what a hypothetical property interest may be worth, providing directional approximation for a set of input characteristics.
Why are land and building values split?
Land does not depreciate, so the difference in value between current and future use reflects differences in time value. A building wears out over long periods of time and passes along in ‘as-is’ condition. Therefore, in addition to greater time value, the life estate gets more benefit from the remaining useful life of the building than the remainder interest does. In order to enjoy the house in its current condition, the remainder interest holder will have to pay for renovations equivalent to the depreciation that occurs in the meantime.
Can all remainder interests be sold?
No, certain remainder interests may be revoked by the life estate holder. This may apply in Texas, Florida, Texas, Michigan, West Virginia, and Vermont. Fractional remainder interests may also be tricky. Local law, property condition, deed terms, and geographic factors will determine whether a remainder interest actually has marketable value.
What is the interest rate assumption?
The calculator relies on a fixed interest rate assumption that I periodically update. It broadly mirrors the IRS Section 7520 rate, with the addition of a funding spread. This spread is included to account for delayed updates (sorry!), an upward sloping U.S. Treasury yield curve, and the difference between market cost of capital and the IRS statutory rate. It incorporates a buffer for illiquidity and unique risks that must be continually monitored, such as total loss from foreclosure if the life estate holder lapses on property tax bills. The funding spread nudges the calculation toward marketable reality, while also discouraging you from inappropriately relying on it for tax purposes.
